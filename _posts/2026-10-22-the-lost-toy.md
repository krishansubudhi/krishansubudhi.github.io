Theo noticed at the bus stop, when he put his hand in his jacket pocket and there was only lint.

"Mama," he said. "Stompy's gone."

Stompy was a green stegosaurus, about as long as a banana, with one plate chipped off his back and teeth marks on his tail from when Theo was three. He had gone to the dentist, to Grandpa's, and once, memorably, into the bathtub. He had never gone missing.

"Check the other pocket," his mother said.

Theo checked the other pocket. He checked his backpack, twice, unzipping every compartment and turning over a squashed granola bar and a mitten that wasn't his. The bus came and he got on without looking up.

At home he started with the obvious places. The couch cushions gave up two pennies and a very old raisin. Under his bed there was a sock, a library book that was eleven days overdue, and no Stompy. He went to the bathroom, the tub, the laundry basket. He went to the kitchen and looked in the cupboard where the pots lived, because once, a long time ago, Stompy had been there, having a meeting.

"Maybe he's hiding," said his little sister Mira, who was four and offered this theory about everything.

"He doesn't hide. He's a dinosaur."

"Dinosaurs hide. They're *big*, so they have to hide *better*."

Theo opened his mouth to argue and found he didn't have the energy. He sat on the kitchen floor with his back against the dishwasher and felt a hot, tight feeling climb up his throat. He was seven, and he had a rule about crying, which was that he did not do it where Mira could see.

His father came home at four and heard the whole story standing in the doorway with his coat still on. He didn't say *it's just a toy*. Theo noticed that, and loved him a little for it. Instead his father took off his coat and said, "Okay. When did you last have him for sure?"

Theo thought. "Breakfast. He was next to my cereal."

"Then at the bus stop he wasn't there. So he's somewhere between your cereal and the bus stop. That's a small area."

It wasn't small, in Theo's opinion, but it did sound like a plan. They retraced the morning. Cereal bowl, the hallway, the front door, and the three steps down. Mira came along with a flashlight, though it was bright out, because a good search needed one. Under the porch steps they found a tennis ball and a snail. At the end of the driveway they found nothing.

They walked the whole way to the bus stop and back, looking in the gutters and the hedges. A neighbor out watering her roses asked what they were looking for, and when Theo said *a green stegosaurus*, she said, very seriously, "Chipped plate on the back?" and then, "No, I'm sorry, I haven't seen him." Theo held onto that. A stranger knew what Stompy looked like. That meant he was real to more than just Theo.

By six o'clock the sun was going orange and the three of them were back on the porch with nothing to show for it. Dinner was rice and eggs, and Theo pushed his around the plate. His mother looked at his father over his head, and nobody said anything about it.

It was Mira who found him, after dinner, when everyone had given up and she had wandered off to put her flashlight away. Theo heard her shout from the front hall, the shout she saved for the really big discoveries, like snow.

She was standing at the coat rack, pointing at his school jacket, the one he had worn that morning and hung up again when he got home. The one he had checked pocket by pocket at the bus stop. But there was a third pocket he'd forgotten about, a small zip one inside the lining, where his mother had once told him to keep important things.

He unzipped it. Stompy was inside, a little warm from the radiator, lying on his side as if he'd been waiting patiently.

Theo had no memory of putting him there. He must have done it at breakfast, half asleep, thinking *keep him safe*. And then forgotten that he'd been the one to keep him safe from himself.

He held the dinosaur in his fist and found, to his surprise, that the hot feeling had gone. In its place was something embarrassed and enormous and light.

"He was hiding," said Mira with great satisfaction. "I *said*."

"You said," Theo agreed.

That night Stompy slept on the pillow instead of at the foot of the bed, which was where he normally went. Theo's father stopped in the doorway to say goodnight and looked at the dinosaur, then at his son.

"Good search," he said.

"Dad. I'm going to put him in the same pocket every time from now on."

"That's a very good system."

"I think I might also tell him where it is," Theo said, and whispered something into the dinosaur's chipped, dented back that his father politely pretended not to hear.
